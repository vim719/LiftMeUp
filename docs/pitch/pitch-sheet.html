1
Explain it like I'm 5

A sticker chart for elevators

Big buildings have elevators. A grown-up called a property manager looks after lots of them.

Every elevator must be checked, and the city must be told. Once a year for one kind of check, once every five years for a bigger one.

Forget, and the city makes you pay. Thousands of dollars for each elevator, and more every month it stays late.

LiftMeUp is the traffic light. It sorts every elevator into red, yellow or green so you fix the red ones first.

"You type in a building's address. LiftMeUp asks the city's public notebook what elevators live there and when they were last checked, then tells you who is late."

2
Problem statement

Commercial property managers risk thousands in fines because no single system tracks fragmented, recurring elevator deadlines across buildings.

84,000+registered elevator and escalator devices under NYC DOB, 63,000+ of them passenger elevators
$3,000fine per elevator for a missed CAT1 (annual) filing, plus $150 per month late
$5,000fine per elevator for a missed CAT5 (five-year) filing, plus $250 per month late

How managers cope today

  • Spreadsheets and paper inspection certificates.
  • Looking up each building by hand on NYC's open-data portal.
  • Checking one building at a time, so nothing is ranked by urgency.

The data is public. What is missing is a portfolio-wide, risk-ranked view of it. Figures from the project's PRD, which cites the NYC DOB Elevator Report.

3
Solution

Type an address. Get a ranked compliance ledger.

Auto-fill from the city

Address becomes a building ID, then the building's elevators and last filing dates are pulled from NYC Open Data. Managers review and override, or add devices by hand.

Due dates, computed live

CAT1 is due 1 year after the last filing, CAT5 after 5 years. Nothing is stored, so editing a date updates the status instantly.

Money at risk

An "Open violation" badge per elevator and a total outstanding fine balance per building, from real city records.

AI briefing

One click gives a short written summary: what is late, what is next, which building to deal with first, with dollar figures.

4
NYC Open Data, complete breakdown

Three city datasets and one city geocoder, no API key

Everything is called from the Django backend over plain HTTPS GET (Python urllib), anonymously, with a 15 second timeout. The browser never talks to NYC directly.

1

Address becomes a building ID (BIN)

NYC PLANNING GEOSEARCH v2 · geosearch.planninglabs.nyc

GET /v2/search?text=350 Fifth Avenue&size=5 returns ranked matches. The BIN sits at properties.addendum.pad.bin. If matches span more than one BIN (for example "200 Water St" in Manhattan and Brooklyn) the user picks one, never the first guess.

2

BIN becomes the building's elevators

DATASET e5aq-a4j2 · DOB NOW: Elevator Safety Compliance

GET /resource/e5aq-a4j2.json?bin=1001686&$limit=1000 returns every device with device_number, status, cat1_latest_report_filed and cat5_latest_report_filed.

3

Devices become editable draft rows

PURE PYTHON, NO NETWORK

Only Active devices are kept. A device with both filing dates becomes two drafts (CAT1 and CAT5) so each ledger row has one inspection type. The manager reviews, overrides and saves.

4

Is this elevator in trouble right now?

DATASET 855j-jady · DOB Safety Violations (device level, updated daily)

$where=device_number in ('1P766',…) AND violation_status='Active' run as one batched call for the whole ledger. A match shows the "Open violation" badge. This feed has no dollar amounts.

5

What does this building owe?

DATASET 6bgk-3dad · DOB ECB Violations (building level, updated weekdays)

$where=bin in ('1001686','1036156',…) in a single call for every building. Rows with balance_due > 0 are summed into the building's outstanding fine total. Open violation count combines 855j-jady and 6bgk-3dad.

6

Plain-English briefing

CLAUDE API · claude-haiku-4-5, temperature 0.2, 300 max tokens

The computed ledger, violation flags and building fine totals are passed to Claude, which writes a short prioritized summary. It only runs on button click, skips the call for an empty portfolio, and a failure returns a graceful "unavailable" message rather than breaking the page.

Judges may ask: why fines per building, not per elevator? The ECB feed has no device identifier, so a per-elevator dollar figure could not be attributed reliably. The team made that a documented product decision: device-level flag from one dataset, building-level dollars from the other.

Engineering details worth a mention

  • Batched queries: one SoQL call for the whole portfolio, so cost stays flat as buildings are added.
  • SoQL values are quote-escaped before being placed in the $where clause.
  • Lookup always returns HTTP 200 with a reason (address_not_found, no_devices_on_file, ambiguous_match, upstream_unavailable) so the UI can fall back to manual entry.
  • The old GeoSearch v1 endpoint now returns HTTP 410; the team found and moved to v2.
  • No Socrata app token needed at demo scale. External calls are mocked in tests.

Beneficiaries and potential clients

Who gets value, and who could pay

Beneficiaries (from the PRD)

  • Property managers running multi-building portfolios: primary users, personally accountable for filings.
  • Building owners: want read-only visibility into their portfolio's compliance.
  • Tenants and the public: fewer lapses in elevator safety paperwork.

Potential clients (our suggestions)

  • Commercial property management firms
  • REITs and real-estate owners with NYC office towers
  • Co-op and condo boards with managing agents
  • Hospitals, universities and hotels with many elevators
  • Elevator maintenance and inspection contractors who file on clients' behalf
  • Insurers and lenders monitoring risk across a book of buildings

The project's docs name managers and owners. The rest are extrapolations for the pitch.

Speaker script · about 280 words

Two minutes, spoken

0:00

New York has over 84,000 registered elevators. Each one has filing deadlines with the city: an annual inspection and a five-year one. Miss one and it's a $3,000 to $5,000 fine per elevator, plus monthly penalties. Property managers track this in spreadsheets and paper, one building at a time.

0:20

LiftMeUp fixes that. Type in a building's address. We find its city building ID, pull its elevators and last filing dates, and instantly rank every elevator red, yellow or green. (Demo: type an address, show the ledger.)

0:45

The data is real, and it's free. We use NYC's geocoder to turn the address into a building number, then three NYC Open Data datasets: Elevator Safety Compliance for devices and filing dates, Safety Violations to flag elevators with an open violation, and ECB Violations to total what each building still owes. No keys, no scraping, and each is one batched call for the whole portfolio.

1:20

It's built with React and TypeScript on the front, Django and Postgres behind it, with every due date computed live so an edit updates the ranking immediately. One click asks Claude to write a short briefing: what's late, what's next, and what it could cost. (Click the AI briefing.)

1:40

Property managers get fewer fines, owners get visibility, and the same approach fits any firm managing elevators across many buildings. The data was always public. We made it useful. We're LiftMeUp.
